project log — photo catalogue

Teaching a Raspberry Pi to know my photo library using AI

pifxui is a JavaFX app that runs headless on a Raspberry Pi 5, catalogues every photo in my cloud storage using local AI models, and lets me find any of them by describing what's in them — a person, a place, a mood — instead of remembering a folder name.

in short

What this actually is, in plain terms

I built an app, from scratch, that looks through my personal photo library, works out who's in each photo, where it was taken, and what's happening in it — then lets me search all of that with a plain sentence, like "photos of Sophie at the beach", instead of scrolling through folders looking for it myself. Whatever comes back can also just play as a slideshow on the small screen or the TV, like a digital photo frame that already knows what you asked for.

The interesting part isn't the photo app — it's how it's built. Rather than send my photos to a service like Google Photos or a cloud AI provider, every part of the "smart" side of this runs on hardware I own, sitting in my house. That meant designing and building all of it myself: the AI that recognises faces and reads a scene, the piece that understands a typed sentence as a search, the software tying it together, and even the small, inexpensive computer it all runs on.

Building it involved:

  • designing and building a complete application end to end — the hardware it runs on, the backend, and the interface
  • working hands-on with modern AI — computer vision and language models — wired into a real, working product, not a one-off demo
  • making it all work within real constraints, on a computer that costs under $100 and is less powerful than a smartphone
  • choosing the harder path of keeping everything private and offline, instead of defaulting to a cloud service
the rig

Small, fanless, and never touched directly

Everything runs on hardware I never actually put my hands on after the initial build. No monitor plugged in for setup, no keyboard — every change goes in over SSH, which shaped a lot of the early decisions about logging and diagnostics.

For anyone who hasn't run into one: a Raspberry Pi is a complete, general-purpose computer — just built onto a single small board, with no case, screen, or peripherals included. A Pi 5 costs well under $200. It's also, by most CPU benchmarks, meaningfully less powerful than a modern smartphone — even an iPhone 13 mini, itself years old at this point, scores roughly three times higher on both single- and multi-core tests (don't worry Pi, I still love you).

the app

One JavaFX app, several small jobs — this post is about one of them

The same JavaFX app runs a few other things on that touchscreen and TV:

  • synced Spotify lyrics for whatever's currently playing (we all love Karaoke!). Spotify doesn't play on the Pi, can be playing anywhere. The app connects to it via Spotify APIs
  • indoor air quality and pollution readings from an Enviro+ sensor
  • a handful of other small utilities

This page is only about the largest piece: the photo AI catalogue — how it processes a photo library and how it answers questions about it.

01 — cataloguing

What happens to a photo the first time it's seen

Cataloguing a directory

Point it at a folder and it walks every photo inside, skipping anything already processed, and extracts a structured record for each one.

What gets extracted per photo

  • Face details, matched against known people in the database
  • EXIF metadata — camera, date, time, GPS, etc
  • Location, resolved from GPS to a real place name. It resolves country, city and region using GPS coordinates
  • Landmarks, when a recognisable one is in frame
  • Description, tags, activities, and general mood
02 — search

Then you can just ask for what you want

Structured search

  • By person — everyone tagged in a photo in a database
  • By landmark — a specific place or monument
  • By location — city, region, or country

Natural language search

Or skip the filters and just describe the photo:

>give all the photos with Sophie
>show me sunset photos from Italy
>find pictures at the beach with the dog
>find pictures with Sophie and Mark at the pool
>find pictures with Sophie at the beach in Greece
>find pictures of people laughing
03 — slideshow

Any search doubles as a slideshow

The slideshow is where all of this actually gets used day to day — the digital-photo-frame side of the app, cycling through photos on the TV or small screen at a fixed interval. What plays isn't fixed to "everything in the library":

  • the whole photo library
  • one specific directory
  • every photo of a given person, or a given location
  • whatever a natural-language search just returned

That last one is the one I use most: search "Sophie and Mark at the beach", then let those results run as a slideshow instead of flipping through them one at a time. Search isn't just a lookup here — it's also how you build an ad-hoc slideshow on the fly.

how it's built

General architecture

The Pi runs the JavaFX front end and a Spring Boot backend side by side, with SQLite holding all the catalogue data. The Pi is capable enough for the app itself and for lighter processing, but running a vision model against every photo would take months to process the whole catalogue — so the heaviest AI work is offloaded to a second machine on the network (still relatively cheap consumer hardware), reachable from Ollama's API, while the Pi stays responsive for the UI.

the ai

Three different kinds of AI, one small board

The catalogue leans on three distinct kinds of AI, each doing a different job: computer vision for face detection and recognition, a vision-language model for scene understanding — tags, mood, landmarks — and a small local LLM for turning a plain-English search into a structured query. Every one of them runs locally, through Ollama, on hardware I own. No photo, no query, and no API call ever leaves the network — there's no cloud AI provider anywhere in the loop, by design, not by accident.

That "by design" matters: the photo library is personal, so sending it through a third-party API was off the table from the start. Local inference also means cataloguing thousands of photos — and re-cataloguing them repeatedly during development, while tuning prompts and comparing models — costs electricity, not a per-token bill. The trade-off is that the hardware has to actually carry the load, which shaped a lot of the engineering below.

The local inference rig

The Pi handles the app, the database, and the lighter local-search model. The vision-language model — the expensive part — runs on a second machine on the same network, reached from the Pi purely through Ollama's API:

Beelink GTi15 Ultra 64GB RAM Intel Arc 140T iGPU Vulkan backend ~99 TOPS

Ollama drives that iGPU through its Vulkan backend, so the vision model runs on-device the same way it would on a discrete GPU, just without one. Two machines, two very different jobs, both entirely local — the split exists purely so a lightweight board can host the app while a heavier model still gets real compute to run on.

Four problems that don't show up in a tutorial

hardware split, kept local A Pi 5 can run the app comfortably, but not a vision-language model against every photo. Rather than reach for a cloud API, the heavy inference runs on the second machine above — still on the local network, still no external call. The Pi stays responsive; the expensive model runs where the hardware actually fits it.
one model loaded at a time Running the vision model and the search model simultaneously would blow the memory budget on modest hardware, so a small model manager swaps which one is loaded based on which job is active — a constraint that mostly disappears if you're calling a hosted API, but is unavoidable the moment inference has to fit on hardware you actually own.
reliable json from a small model Natural-language search depends on a 3B-parameter local model returning valid, schema-shaped JSON every time — no small ask for a model that size. The fix that mattered most was marking optional fields as required-but-nullable in the schema: an optional field, a small model will often skip outright; a required nullable one, it reliably fills in or explicitly nulls.
evaluating models on the real task Picking each model meant testing candidates head to head on actual queries and actual hardware, not benchmark scores. The first vision model tried covered structured labels correctly only about 56% of the time; a search-model candidate that looked fine on paper OOM-crashed the Pi at run time. The models that shipped were the ones that held up under those conditions, not the newest ones.
under the hood

How faces get matched to people

Detection runs through InsightFace's buffalo_l model — a RetinaFace detector as the primary pass, with an OpenCV Haar-cascade fallback for the frames RetinaFace misses. (Worth noting: buffalo_l ended up outperforming the newer antelopev2 model in practice, which isn't what the version numbers suggest.) Every photo is corrected for EXIF orientation before detection, since a sideways photo throws bounding boxes off completely, and the raw detections are cleaned up afterwards — overlapping boxes merged by IoU, and implausible ones dropped by aspect ratio, since a detector run over real photos always returns some noise.

Each surviving face becomes a numeric embedding — a fixed-length vector fingerprint of that face, not a stored image. Recognition happens entirely in that vector space: each known person is represented by an L2-normalised centroid of their embeddings, and a new face is matched by cosine distance to the nearest centroid rather than by comparing pixels at all.

The matching itself is deliberately cautious. Small, newly-formed clusters use a stricter distance threshold than established ones, so one weak match early on can't drag a person's whole cluster off course. Timestamps factor in too — a candidate face closer in time to a person's existing photos is treated as a somewhat likelier match, which helps separate two people who genuinely look alike. None of it is treated as final: faces can be reassigned, merged into another cluster, or excluded from matching entirely — useful for a mask or a bad crop that shouldn't pull a centroid off center — without being deleted from the catalogue.

under the hood

How the natural language search works

A typed query never touches the photo library directly — it's first handed to a small local language model, running through Ollama, whose only job is to turn a sentence into a structured request: which person, which place, which tags or landmarks, what kind of mood or activity.

That structured request is then run against the catalogue like any other filtered search. So "beach photos with the dog last summer" resolves to a location hint, a tag, and a date range before a single photo is looked at — the language model's only job is translation, not the search itself.
